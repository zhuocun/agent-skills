# Risk Checks

Use a section only when its trigger appears in the change. Inside a section, apply only the checks the code makes reachable. For each check you apply, note the trigger, the contract or sourced limit, the evidence and the result, or the proof that is missing. Report a defect only through the verification and threshold in the main skill. These checks grant no permission to run operational actions or change policy. Prefer the project's existing mechanisms; do not recommend a new facility when an existing one covers the need.

## Values, Time and Collections

- **Clocks across machines.** When expiry, leases, scheduling or cleanup compares times from different machines, find the time authority and the allowed clock skew. Check behavior when a clock jumps forward or back. Local timestamp order does not prove global order. Do not let skew silently extend an access lifetime past its contract.
- **Elapsed time.** Measure local timeouts and budgets with a monotonic clock, not wall-clock time.
- **Calendar arithmetic.** Use calendar operations for days, months and years; a fixed 24-hour duration is wrong across daylight-saving changes.
- **Numbers in transit.** Trace input, computation, conversion and storage. Check that the stored value still meets the column's type, scale and constraint, and that a conversion does not bypass validation. Check exponent forms (`1e3`) and overflow where input is parsed. Do not demand exact decimal arithmetic for values that are not money or counts.
- **Missing and empty values.** Distinguish a missing field, explicit null, an empty list, blank text, a default and a display value. A read-then-write of a whole record must not drop fields the code does not understand.
- **Collection identity and limits.** Check the identity used for deduplication (all parts of a compound key), how nulls are counted, per-group versus overall limits, and exact-limit and limit-plus-one cases. Splitting a batch must not change the result for the whole set.

## Concurrency and Progress

- **Cross-row invariants.** For a rule that spans rows or ranges (no overlapping bookings, a total under a cap, "no active record exists"), list what the decision read and what protects each read fact until commit. Two transactions can both pass the check and both write (write skew). One row's version or a snapshot read does not protect a rule across rows. A guarantee for one record does not cover several records, partitions or replicas.
- **Locks and waits.** When work takes locks, connections, pool slots, leases or waits on child work, trace the order of acquisition. Check for cycles, for work that waits on a task needing the slot it holds, and for starvation. Repeated timeouts do not prove progress. Do not add coordination to isolated, immutable computation.
- **Deduplication facilities.** When the contract requires that an effect happen once, check the key's scope (operation and caller), that the key is bound to the content that affects the result, ownership under concurrent arrival, the in-progress state, storage of the result, and retention covering the promised replay window. Do not recommend a deduplication facility where no requirement asks for one.

## Asynchronous Work and Recovery

- **Authority for async work.** Distinguish a user request, a delayed command acting for a user, and a notice of a fact that already happened. Check the producer is the expected one and the service identity, target tenant and delegation scope are right. A tenant or user ID in the payload is not authority. For delayed commands, check what the contract says about permission changes, revocation and expiry between request and execution. For completed-fact notices, check origin and permission to process; do not demand that the original user still holds the permission to start it.
- **Acknowledgment.** List in order: ack, consumer progress, business effect, retry handoff, final-failure handling. When the contract promises durable handling, the effect or a durable handoff must happen before the ack. A function return, an in-memory queue or a fire-and-forget submit is not durable. Check partial batches and a crash at each step.
- **Order.** For commands or events that do not commute, find the required order, the routing key, the scope the broker actually orders within, and the sequence evidence. Check gaps, reordering and late arrivals against the contract: wait, reject, reconcile or recover. Partitions and timestamps do not prove global order. Do not demand ordering for effects that commute.
- **Stale writes.** A consumer applying a snapshot must not overwrite a newer one, and must not drop increments the contract keeps.
- **Expired ownership.** When a worker's lease can expire, the final write and the lease release must check the worker's generation or token, so a stale worker cannot write or release its successor's lease.
- **Recovery after a crash.** Mark each interruption point: external call, local commit, outcome record, compensation, notification. Where recovery is required, the operation's identity and intent must survive a process crash or be rebuilt from durable data. Distinguish not executed, executed and unknown, including failed and unknown compensation. Manual recovery needs a detection condition, the evidence, an entry point and executable steps; a named owner alone is not enough. Keep an accepted loss window visible instead of inventing a stronger promise.
- **Resource lifetime.** Trace owned resources (connections, files, threads, streams) through errors, early returns, nested scopes and shutdown. Owned resources must close; borrowed ones stay open for their owner. Restore request context after a nested scope. Check that failures of async work are observed, not dropped.

## Budgets and Capacity

- **Executed queries.** At the largest allowed input size, check the statements actually executed: bindings and their types, predicates, returned volume, query count and the plan where available. Apply authorization before counts and paging. Builder code, an index declaration or a tiny sample is not evidence of how a query runs.
- **Remaining time.** Subtract time spent queuing, processing and backing off from the original request's budget. Before new work or a retry, recompute what remains and pass a deadline on. With no time left, start no new requested work and take the contracted failure or fallback. Required cleanup, cancellation or reconciliation of an unknown outcome may still run, under its own bounded budget or a durable handoff, but it must not extend the caller's deadline or start new requested effects. A retry does not get a fresh full budget. A tiny remaining value must not be passed as zero where zero means "no limit".
- **Total cost.** Where resource use changes, note the request rate, concurrency, payload size and depth, and data skew, with their sources. Trace parsing, decompression, serialization, lock hold time and retained results. Put limits before expensive work. Judge total amplification, not only per-item or per-page cost. Do not require a full load test for an unrelated change.
- **Overload and retries.** Multiply attempts across the whole call chain. Check concurrency limits, queue sizes, waits and backoff against real resource limits, and add jitter where many clients retry together. Work still running after a timeout still uses resources. Propagate cancellation where supported, and do not claim it stopped effects where it does not. Failing fast with no retry can be correct. Do not add retries, circuit breakers or a rate-limit service by default.

## Caches and Diagnostics

- **Existing caches.** The key must include every input that changes the result, including the caller's scope. Distinguish cached success, cached "not found", cached dependency failure, expired and approved-stale entries. Check invalidation after writes and rollbacks, and that a slow fill cannot overwrite newer data. Expiry alone does not make data fresh. A shared cache must not replace a required per-request authorization check. Do not recommend a cache for request-local reuse.
- **Offline data investigation.** When an authorized investigation reads stored data, keep invalid raw values without widening the valid domain states. Record each row anomaly that can be judged on its own and continue with the other rows; stop on connection failure or when a decisive fact is unknown. Online writes must still reject corrupt data. Do not repair the investigated data without permission.

## Data Protection and Technical Operations

- **Values reaching technical operations.** Trace client-supplied values into SQL, shell commands, file paths, URLs, templates, and deserialization. Each must arrive as a bound parameter, an escaped value or a value checked against an allowed set, under the real execution rules. Login, business validation or a string check elsewhere does not make the value safe for that operation. A dynamic target (file path, outbound URL, class name) must be restricted to the allowed set. Do not demand blanket sanitization of every ordinary field.
- **Personal and secret data.** Trace where such data goes: responses, logs, caches, messages, samples, backups and deletion paths. Check that a debug setting cannot turn off peer authentication or transport encryption. Secrets must be replaceable, not hard-coded. Use the project's approved classification and existing mechanisms; do not invent cryptography or apply one protection level to all data.

## Migration, Release and Configuration

- **Live migration of retained data.** Check lock duration, load, waits imposed on business traffic, writes arriving during the migration, progress tracking, bounded batches, safe re-entry after interruption and a stop condition. A backfill must not overwrite newer data. Reconcile business invariants, not only row counts. Empty-store setup or an approved small offline migration can be simple. Do not override an explicit decision not to migrate.
- **Destructive conversion.** Identify the irreversible step and the promised recovery point, recovery time and allowed loss. Check that the original form, a usable copy or a lossless reverse transformation exists and survives later writes. A backup setting is not proof of restore. Recovery must not overwrite later legitimate writes. Run recovery drills only on isolated resources you are allowed to use.
- **Release and rollback.** Distinguish "process started", "ready for traffic" and "business correct". Check the stop condition, every reachable mix of code, configuration, data and message versions, and in-flight work during switchover and shutdown. Call rollback viable only with evidence that the old version reads new data and handles outstanding work; otherwise record the limit and a forward fix. Never delete new data to make rollback look possible.
- **Configuration changes.** Check valid, default, missing and invalid values, and both branches of each switch. For live changes, check which configuration version a request uses, which mixes are safe, and what happens to queued work. Turning a switch off must stop the forbidden effect; turning it back on does not undo durable effects. Validate static settings at startup. Do not recommend dynamic configuration or flags without a requirement.

## Compatibility Evidence

- **Real encoders and decoders.** For a protocol or format change, test with versioned actual encoders and decoders: old sender with new receiver and new sender with old receiver, for requests and responses. Assert that an old decoder skips each added field where the protocol allows added fields, or show that none was added. Check types, IDs, units, enum values, unknown fields, and every old entry still reachable against the new checks. Where the protocol requires version negotiation or approved rejection instead, check that.
- **How long compatibility must last.** Derive the needed combinations from real client lifetimes, rolling-deploy overlap, retained and replayable messages, queued jobs, stored formats and promised backup restores. Deploy overlap does not cover a longer retention promise.
- **Released behavior.** Bind a "behavior preserved" claim to the immutable released artifact or an approved equivalent, and record its identity. Compare inputs, outputs, defaults, failures, ordering and side effects. Keep this comparison a separate acceptance gate even when source or codec checks pass; if it is unavailable, name the owner and the gate it must pass before. For a first release, state why the comparison does not apply.
- **Samples.** Produce responses with the delivered producer code and requests with the actual client encoding, and record where samples came from. Contract-derived seed data run through real code in an isolated environment is valid when labeled honestly. Handwritten samples alone do not prove the chain.
- **Dependencies of the artifact.** Check dependency declarations, version authority, packaging and the target runtime for affected artifacts, and build or package them. A host compile or a matching interface name does not prove runtime compatibility.

## Logs and Detection

- **Useful, safe logs.** At the final error boundary, check the log carries the failure class, safe identifiers and the reason, so events can be correlated. Logs must not carry secrets or whole sensitive payloads, must strip control characters from client values, and must bound field size. One central log is enough; every layer need not log the same event.
- **Detecting silent failure.** Apply when a sourced detection duty exists, or when a silent failure could hide an accepted write, an external effect or a completed job. Name the signal, the condition that raises it, the window and the response owner. Distinguish zero traffic, zero errors and a missing signal, and cover "no progress". If no detection time is sourced, record the missing owner decision. A metric existing does not prove business health. Do not instrument every pure function.

## Dependency Upgrades

- **Changed behavior.** List direct and transitive changes and read the relevant release notes. Check changes in serialization, time handling, retries, transactions, exceptions and connections, and that the build is reproducible. Relate published vulnerabilities to reachable runtime conditions and leave unknown exposure marked unknown. A successful compile or a small version bump does not prove unchanged behavior. Do not upgrade unrelated dependencies.

## Test Evidence

- **Independent expectations.** Expected values must come from the contract, not from the code's output. Positive, negative and boundary cases must check the exact error, the full result and the required effects.
- **Fault sensitivity.** When a changed guard or calculation controls authorization, stored data or an irreversible effect, pick a few relevant faults (flipped boundary, deleted guard, wrong classification, missing effect) and check a test fails for each, by reasoning or by an authorized isolated mutation. One caught fault does not prove the suite is adequate.
- **Concurrency tests.** Use distinguishable writes and explicit states to drive success, conflict, abort, and confirmed and unknown commit, then check final contents and row counts. Waits must be bounded and fail on timeout. Fixed sleeps and identical payloads do not prove the intended interleaving. Check storage-specific claims on the real storage engine and settings.
- **Dependency failures.** Drive scripted failures through the real adapter up to the entry point. Check call budgets, calls that must not happen, unexpected calls and unused scripted responses. A stub that always succeeds does not test failure handling.
- **Execution claims.** For each claimed result, keep the command, revision, selected tests, outcome and whether artifacts were fresh. Reading code, in-memory stores or a local run cannot prove behavior on the target infrastructure.
